# From Discovery to Requirements

![From Discovery to Requirements](/images/pages/guides/discovery-to-requirements.png)

Discovery produces understanding. But understanding in people's heads isn't useful to the rest of the process. It needs to be externalized. That externalization is the requirement.

## When Requirements Are "Good Enough"

Requirements don't have to be perfect. They have to be good enough to design from. Good enough means:

The problem is articulated clearly. Someone who wasn't in the room can read the requirement and understand what problem is being solved.

The scope is bounded. You know what's in and what's out. Not every edge case, but the general boundaries.

The stakeholders agree. Not on the solution (that's design), but on the problem. Everyone acknowledges this is a real problem worth solving.

If you have those three things, you can move to Design. You'll come back to refine requirements as you learn more. That's the lifecycle working correctly.

## What Requirements Look Like

They can be simple sentences: "Customers who visit an out-of-stock product page have no way to know when the item returns."

They can be structured: using formats like Jobs-to-be-Done ("When I find a book I want but it's out of stock, I want to be notified when it's available, so I don't have to keep checking manually").

They can be visual: journey maps showing where the breakdown happens, empathy maps showing what the user thinks and feels at the point of frustration.

They can come from data: "37% of users who view out-of-stock pages never return to the site. Of the 63% who do return, the average time to return is 14 days."

The format matters less than the clarity. A clear sentence beats a sloppy canvas. A good canvas beats a vague paragraph.

> 🔄 **Feedback opportunity:** Share your requirements with someone who'll push back. A skeptic is more useful than a supporter at this stage. You want to hear "but what about..." and "are you sure that's the real problem?" before you invest in designing a solution.

## The Handoff That Isn't a Handoff

In traditional processes, requirements get "handed off" to a design team. The discovery team writes requirements, throws them over a wall, and the design team works from them.

This doesn't work well because requirements always have implicit assumptions that only become visible when someone tries to design a solution from them. The handoff creates a gap where context gets lost.

The better approach: the people who did discovery participate in the design phase. They don't have to do the design work, but they should be in the room when the requirements get translated into solutions. Their context fills the gaps that no document can fully capture.

This is one of the core arguments for cross-functional teams. Not because everyone should do everything, but because the transitions between phases lose context when they're also transitions between people.
