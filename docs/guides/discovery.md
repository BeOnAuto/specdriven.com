# Where Specs Come From

![Where Specs Come From](/images/pages/guides/discovery.png)

Specifications don't come from nowhere. They come from design. And design comes from understanding the problem. If you skip problem understanding, you'll design the wrong solution. If you skip design, you'll write specifications for something nobody thought through.

Discovery is the work that happens before anyone writes a spec.

## Three Activities

Discovery has three activities, and they often overlap and interleave. But it's useful to name them.

**Ideation.** Coming up with possibilities. What could we build? What problems could we solve? What opportunities exist? This can happen alone (going for a walk, prepping your mind before sleep, sketching on paper) or in groups (brainstorming sessions, hackathons, whiteboard conversations).

Group ideation has a built-in quality function: the discussion surfaces good ideas and filters bad ones through the discourse itself. But it can also suppress ideas from quieter team members. A good facilitator alternates between individual thinking time and group discussion so both dynamics contribute.

**Research.** Validating ideas against reality through market research, competitor analysis, user interviews, data analysis, customer conversations, analytics review, or just reading about what others have tried.

Research is where you find out if the problem you identified actually exists, and how badly people want it solved. You might discover the problem is already solved by a competitor. You might discover the problem is bigger than you thought. Both are valuable feedback.

**Conceptualization.** Giving structure and language to what you've found. Naming the problem. Defining who it affects. Establishing the vocabulary the team will use. This is where tools like the Lean Canvas, Business Model Canvas, or empathy mapping help. They give you frameworks to organize your thinking.

Conceptualization is the bridge into the design phase. When you can articulate the problem clearly, define who it's for, and describe the constraints and context, you have requirements. Requirements are the output of Discovery.

> 🔄 **Feedback opportunity:** Can you state your requirement in one sentence that a stranger would understand? If it takes a paragraph, you haven't conceptualized it clearly enough. Try explaining it to someone outside the team. Their questions are pure gold.

## Requirements Are Problem-Space Artifacts

Requirements describe problems, not solutions. This matters because the language you use shapes the thinking that follows.

"We need a notification system" is not a requirement. It's a solution dressed up as a requirement. It skips the problem entirely.

"We're losing sales because customers leave when items are out of stock and have no way to come back when items return" is a requirement. It describes the problem. It leaves the solution open. Maybe the solution is notifications. Maybe it's better inventory management. Maybe it's showing alternatives. The requirement doesn't constrain the solution space.

When requirements accidentally include solution language, they close off design options before the design phase even starts. Teams end up building what was described in the requirement rather than the best solution to the actual problem.

> 🔄 **Feedback opportunity:** Read your requirements and highlight any solution language. Words like "button," "page," "email," "API," "database" in a requirement are signals that you've jumped to solution before finishing discovery. Rewrite the requirement in problem-only language and see if it changes what solutions you consider.

## The Non-Linearity

Discovery feeds Design, but Design also feeds Discovery. You'll start designing a solution and realize you don't understand part of the problem. That's feedback pulling you back to Discovery. That's healthy.

The general movement should be forward: from understanding the problem to designing the solution. But expect to loop back. Expect late discoveries. Expect "we didn't think of that" moments. Each one is a feedback opportunity, not a failure.

The teams that struggle aren't the ones who loop back. They're the ones who never loop back because they never checked.
